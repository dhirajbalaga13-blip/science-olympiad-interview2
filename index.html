Beginner's Guide to Interviewing

This guide is designed to help interviewers conduct consistent, fair, useful Science Olympiad interviews.

1. Before the interview

  • Read the applicant's name and grade before starting.
  • Know the questions ahead of time.
  • Try to give each applicant approximately the same amount of time.
  • Use the same core questions for everyone.
  • Have follow-up questions ready, but do not turn them into a different interview for every person.
  • Focus on evidence rather than your first impression.

2. Start comfortably

Interviews can be stressful, especially for students who have never done one before. A nervous applicant may need a minute to settle in.

  • Introduce yourself.
  • Explain roughly how the interview will work.
  • Make sure the applicant understands that they can take a moment to think.
  • Do not intentionally make the applicant uncomfortable.

3. Use follow-up questions

A good follow-up asks the applicant to explain something they already mentioned.

Good examples:

  • "What happened next?"
  • "What did you learn from that?"
  • "Why did you choose that approach?"
  • "Can you give me an example?"
  • "What would you do differently next time?"

Avoid follow-ups that effectively give one applicant an advantage over another by coaching them toward the answer you want.

4. What to look for

Strong interviews do not necessarily come from students who already know the most Science Olympiad facts. Look for evidence of qualities that can contribute to a team.

  • Interest: Does the applicant actually want to participate?
  • Curiosity: Do they enjoy learning or exploring things?
  • Problem solving: Can they describe how they handle setbacks?
  • Creativity: Can they approach ideas from different angles?
  • Team fit: Do they understand that Science Olympiad is collaborative?
  • Communication: Can they explain their thinking?

5. Rating scale

1
Very weak evidence
The interview provides little evidence of the quality.
2
Below average evidence
Some evidence exists, but there are meaningful weaknesses.
3
Solid / average evidence
The applicant demonstrates the quality adequately.
4
Strong evidence
The applicant provides several convincing examples.
5
Exceptional evidence
The applicant gives unusually strong, specific, or insightful evidence.

6. Do not confuse nervousness with weakness

A student may speak quietly, pause before answering, fidget, forget a word, or appear uncomfortable. None of those things automatically means the student lacks curiosity, intelligence, teamwork ability, or motivation.

Look at the substance of the applicant's answer. If the applicant gives a thoughtful answer after taking a moment to think, that is still thoughtful reasoning.

7. Body language and interview observations

Body language should never become a hidden personality test. Eye contact, posture, facial expression, movement, speaking speed, and other behaviors vary widely between individuals.

The observation checkboxes in this application are primarily intended to help interviewers remember concrete things that happened during the interview. They should not automatically increase or decrease an applicant's score.

Whenever possible, record observable evidence rather than interpretations.

For example:

  • Better: "Asked how the team handles disagreements."
  • Less useful: "Seems like a natural leader."

8. Red flags vs. ordinary nervousness

One awkward answer should not determine an applicant's entire evaluation.

If something concerns you, look for a pattern or specific evidence rather than relying on intuition.

  • Was the issue actually relevant to team participation?
  • Did the applicant explain the situation?
  • Did they demonstrate reflection or willingness to improve?
  • Would you judge the same behavior the same way in another applicant?

9. Avoid bias

  • Do not favor students because they remind you of yourself.
  • Do not assume previous Science Olympiad experience automatically means someone is a better applicant.
  • Do not reward confidence alone.
  • Do not penalize shyness alone.
  • Do not make decisions based on appearance.
  • Do not use stereotypes about grade level, personality, or background.
  • Use the same standards for every applicant.

10. Write useful notes

Good notes help the team remember why a score was given later.

Instead of:

"Really smart."

Try:

"Explained how they changed their approach after their first experiment failed and identified what they would test differently."

11. After the interview

  • Finish your ratings while the interview is fresh.
  • Write down important evidence.
  • Avoid changing scores simply because another interviewer liked or disliked someone.
  • When comparing applicants, compare the evidence and criteria—not just overall impressions.
  • Back up the database regularly.

12. The most important rule

Evaluate what the applicant demonstrates, not what you assume about the applicant.

The goal of an interview is to gather useful evidence, not to find the person who is the most charismatic for ten minutes.
